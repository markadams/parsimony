\begin{abstract}
Automated floating-point precision tuning was solved, in method, over a decade
ago. Precimonious~\cite{precimonious} searched type configurations with delta
debugging; Blame Analysis~\cite{blame} made the search tractable by pruning it
with a single shadow execution. Both are unmaintained, and we argue the reason is not
that the methods failed but that the hardware of the time did not reward
them: in the Blame Analysis evaluation, four of ten benchmark programs show
exactly $0.0\%$ speedup at every error threshold tested, and a fifth never
exceeds $0.4\%$. Converting \texttt{double} to \texttt{float} on a 2013 CPU
bought a little vectorization and cache residency, net of the casts it
introduced, and the tools frequently reported that the original program was
fastest.

The objective function has since changed. Reduced precision now addresses
\texttt{bfloat16} and 8-bit storage formats and bandwidth-bound kernels, in which
narrowing a matrix's storage cuts memory traffic --- though by less than the
format width suggests, for reasons we quantify, and not at all in some cases we
measure. Tensor cores, the usual motivation for mixed precision, play no part
here: they need dense tiles far larger than a small sparse block, and our kernels
issue no matrix instructions. We present
\emph{parsimony}, a precision-tuning framework that separates the two concerns
prior tools fused --- how a precision configuration is \emph{evaluated}, and how
the configuration space is \emph{searched} --- and reimplements the delta-debugging
search against modern formats. We validate the emulation against hardware and the
search against a synthetic oracle, and reproduce a published Blame Analysis result
at a finer granularity than the original tool could express.

On a conjugate-gradient solver the answer is structured rather than uniform: the
scalars that steer the iteration ($\alpha$, $\beta$, $\rho$) tolerate
\texttt{bfloat16} and below, while the operator and the state vectors set the
achievable accuracy, with \texttt{fp32} operator storage sufficient at
$10^{-6}$ --- the decision that matters most for a bandwidth-bound solver. We
contribute a prefilter establishing per-variable floors in $O(n \log L)$
evaluations, which cuts the search by $6$--$15\times$ for at most $8\%$ more
storage, and we report three experimental-design failures encountered in
producing these numbers, each of which yields a plausible but meaningless result.
For Newton's method we argue that the right observable is iteration count at
fixed residual tolerance, since a stored Jacobian cannot change the fixed point,
and measure that \texttt{fp32} Jacobian storage costs no iterations, \texttt{fp16}
costs a third more, and below about 10 bits Newton degrades to Picard iteration
rather than diverging. The same residual may be stored in 16 bits but not
evaluated in fewer than 33 --- storage precision and arithmetic precision are
different questions, and a solver that evaluates its residual in low precision
reports false convergence as fast convergence.
Measuring the payoff on an A100 sharpens rather than confirms our own thesis.
Storing the operator in \texttt{fp32} costs no iterations and, in a blocked
layout, runs each sparse matrix-vector product $2.86\times$ faster than the scalar
\texttt{fp64} a solver is likely using --- but on a scalar seven-point stencil
the same precision change yields a $1.07\times$ speedup, which is the 2013
result reproduced
on 2026 hardware. The payoff comes from the blocked format at least as much as
from the hardware, and a tool reporting ``\texttt{fp32} suffices'' has not
predicted a speedup, because the same answer is worth a $2.86\times$ or a
$1.07\times$ speedup depending on a storage decision the tool never sees.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Choosing floating-point precision is a decision most scientific software makes
once, conservatively, and never revisits. Declaring everything \texttt{double} is
defensible, cheap to reason about, and --- on the hardware most of this software
was written for --- nearly free. That last clause no longer holds.

Automated tuning tools for this problem appeared a decade ago and worked.
Precimonious~\cite{precimonious} treats each floating-point variable as taking a
type from $\{\texttt{float}, \texttt{double}, \texttt{long double}\}$ and searches
that space with Zeller and Hildebrandt's delta-debugging
algorithm~\cite{ddmin}, seeking an assignment that both meets an accuracy
threshold and runs faster. Blame Analysis~\cite{blame} attacks the resulting
scalability problem: rather than recompiling and rerunning per candidate, it
executes the program once under shadow execution, computing for each instruction
the minimum operand precision that still yields the required result precision,
and uses the result to prune Precimonious' search space --- $9\times$ faster
analysis on average, up to $38\times$.

Neither tool is maintained. The natural reading is that the approach failed. We
think the evidence supports a different reading, and it is visible in the
tools' own numbers.

\subsection{The observation}

Table~5 of the Blame Analysis paper reports program speedup after tuning, across
ten programs and four error thresholds ($10^{-4}$ through $10^{-10}$), for both
Precimonious alone and Blame\,+\,Precimonious. For \texttt{bessel},
\texttt{gaussian}, \texttt{roots}, and \texttt{rootnewt}, every entry is
$0.0\%$. For \texttt{polyroots} no entry exceeds $0.4\%$. Real speedups appear
only for \texttt{sum} ($39.9\%$), \texttt{fft} ($8.3\%$), \texttt{blas}
($5.1\%$), NAS \texttt{cg} ($7.7$--$7.9\%$), and \texttt{ep} ($0.6\%$). In the
configuration tables, a majority of cells are marked to indicate that the tools
selected the \emph{original} program as fastest.

The picture is starker still at the tighter thresholds, where the accuracy
demanded is closest to what a production solver requires. At $10^{-8}$ and
$10^{-10}$, \texttt{fft} and \texttt{blas} join the first four at $0.0\%$ and
\texttt{sum} follows at $10^{-10}$; for Precimonious alone, \emph{exactly one} of
the ten programs --- NAS \texttt{cg}, at $7.4$--$7.9\%$ --- shows any speedup at
all.

The zero speedups are not a failure of search. The searches found valid
lower-precision
configurations --- Blame Analysis reports that on average $40\%$ of program
variables can be declared \texttt{float} (median $28\%$). It is a failure of
\emph{payoff}. On a 2013 general-purpose CPU, both \texttt{float} and
\texttt{double} arithmetic issue at similar rates; the gains available are
narrower vector lanes and better cache residency, and they are offset by the
conversion instructions a mixed configuration introduces. The Blame Analysis
authors say so directly, noting that lowering precision does not necessarily
produce a faster program and that a triage by the programmer is required.

\subsection{Why this is now a different problem}

Three changes since 2016 alter the arithmetic of that trade-off.

\paragraph{Formats below \texttt{float}.} The tuning lattice of the original
tools was $\{\texttt{float}, \texttt{double}, \texttt{long double}\}$, with
intermediate \emph{virtual} precisions used internally by Blame Analysis but not
expressible as storage. Hardware now provides \texttt{bfloat16}, \texttt{fp16},
\texttt{tf32}, and two 8-bit encodings. A variable that Precimonious could at
best move from 64 to 32 bits may now move to 8, and the search space is
correspondingly richer.

\paragraph{Throughput that now differs by format.} Matrix units on current
accelerators deliver order-of-magnitude higher throughput at reduced precision,
and that throughput is what most often motivates mixed precision. It is worth
saying plainly
that it is \emph{not} the mechanism at work in this paper. Tensor cores operate on
dense tiles --- $16\times16\times16$ at smallest for the half-precision
instruction --- so a $3\times3$ elasticity block fills nine of 256 slots, and
NVIDIA's own tensor-core sparse path requires block sizes of
16 or more for that reason. Our kernels use no matrix instructions at all;
inspection of the generated assembly finds zero \texttt{HMMA} and only scalar
\texttt{DFMA}. Reduced precision here is a \emph{storage} decision, and the
payoff we measure is memory traffic, not arithmetic throughput. For small-block
sparse solvers, that is the honest framing: the matrix units cannot be used at this
block size, and the memory bandwidth can.

\paragraph{Bandwidth-bound kernels.} Much of scientific computing --- sparse
matrix-vector products above all --- is limited by memory traffic rather than by
arithmetic. For these, precision is a \emph{storage} decision whose payoff does
not depend on what the arithmetic units do. This case barely existed in the
original benchmark set, drawn from GSL routines and small NAS kernels.

How much traffic a narrower value format saves depends on the sparse data
structure, not only on the format,
and the arithmetic is worth doing once. In block CSR with block size $b$, one
sparse matrix-vector product moves
\[
  r_b\,(b^2 w + 4) \;+\; 4 \;+\; b\,(v + 8) \quad\text{bytes per block row},
\]
for $r_b$ blocks per block row, value width $w$ and vector width $v$. Narrowing
$w$ leaves three terms untouched: the column indices, the row pointer, and the
vector read and write. Since a block carries one index for $b^2$ values, the
index cost is $4/b^2$ bytes per scalar nonzero, and the factor by which traffic
shrinks is bounded by
\[
  \frac{8 + 4/b^2}{w + 4/b^2}.
\]

At $b=1$ --- scalar CSR, PETSc's \texttt{AIJ} --- this is $1.5\times$ for
\texttt{fp32} and $2\times$ for \texttt{fp16}, \emph{not} $2\times$ and
$4\times$: a 4-byte column index per nonzero caps it, and the bound is approached
only asymptotically, as the number of nonzeros per row grows. At a seven-point
stencil, with the row-pointer and vector terms included, the model gives
$1.36\times$ and $1.67\times$.

Blocking raises the bound materially. At $b=3$ the index costs $4/9$ of a byte per
scalar nonzero and the bound rises to $1.90\times$ and $3.45\times$. We take the
block size and stencil from PETSc's \texttt{snes/tutorials/ex56}, 3D linear
elasticity on hexahedra, by measurement rather than assumption: PETSc reports
$b = 3$, and on a $d^3$ grid of interior nodes the assembled block count is
$(3d-2)^3$ for trilinear elements and $(4d-5)^3$ for triquadratic ones, closed
forms that reproduce every measured run exactly. Blocks per block row therefore
tend to $27$ and $64$ respectively.

At the trilinear stencil the byte model gives traffic reductions of
$1.85\times$ (\texttt{fp32}) and $3.24\times$ (\texttt{fp16}) in \texttt{BAIJ},
against $1.48\times$ and $1.96\times$ for the identical matrix stored as
\texttt{AIJ}. Blocking alone, before any precision change, already cuts traffic
by $1.41\times$, and \texttt{AIJ} at \texttt{fp64} moves $2.62\times$ the bytes
of \texttt{BAIJ} at \texttt{fp32}. The conclusion is insensitive to the stencil:
across both element orders and every mesh measured, that ratio stays within
$2.62$--$2.67\times$, because at $b = 3$ the index term is already small.
Narrowing the \emph{vector} rather than
the matrix buys under $7\%$ in either layout, its traffic being $O(n)$ against
the matrix's $O(\mathrm{nnz})$.

Table~\ref{tab:spmv} is the measurement, on one A100. Three things in it bound
every performance claim in this paper.

One caveat governs the whole table, and \S\ref{sec:e2e} makes it concrete. These
are two kernels we wrote, differing only in layout, so the comparison isolates
the layout cleanly --- but it does not predict what a production library will do
with the same two layouts. PETSc's blocked device kernel takes $1.49\times$
the time of its scalar one on a single A100, where our blocked kernel is
$1.64\times$ \emph{faster} than our scalar one, because its scalar path is more
heavily tuned and because the
blocked format's index saving is only realized once there is halo communication
to amortize it against~\cite{baijkokkos}. Table~\ref{tab:spmv} measures what blocking
is worth in bytes and in one kernel; it does not measure what any particular
implementation extracts from it.

\emph{Blocked \texttt{fp32} is the real win, and it beats the model.} It realizes
$87\%$ of its predicted gain, and against scalar \texttt{fp64} --- what a PETSc
user has today --- it delivers a $2.86\times$ speedup where the byte model
predicted $2.63\times$. Blocking over-delivers because it does not only move
fewer bytes,
it moves them better: $77\%$ of peak bandwidth against $67\%$ for the scalar
layout, from fewer and more regular loads.

\emph{The 2013 result survives for scalar formats.} On a seven-point stencil,
\texttt{fp32} yields a $1.07\times$ speedup --- $19\%$ of its predicted
$1.37\times$, which is to say nothing. That is the same ``do not bother'' the
Blame Analysis
evaluation reported, on 2026 hardware: at seven nonzeros per row the kernel is
latency-bound rather than bandwidth-bound, and narrowing the values removes
traffic the kernel was not waiting on. This sharpens the thesis of
\S\ref{sec:intro} rather than confirming it. It
is not simply that the hardware changed. On the scalar formats the original tools
targeted, reduced precision still buys almost nothing on a thin stencil; what
unlocks the payoff is the blocked format, a data-structure choice.

\emph{\texttt{fp16} underdelivers everywhere}, realizing $58\%$ even blocked.
Achieved bandwidth falls from 1573 to 1115 GB/s --- less data moved, but not
proportionally faster --- so at values narrower than four bytes the kernel has
left the
bandwidth-bound regime and per-element instruction cost dominates. Some of that
may be our kernel rather than the hardware, and we do not claim this is the best
achievable \texttt{fp16} figure; but the direction is unambiguous, and it makes
the format-width ratio an upper bound that \texttt{fp32} approaches and
\texttt{fp16} does not.

Two conclusions follow. Reduced precision and blocking are complementary rather
than alternative ways to cut traffic, and the combination is worth more than
either alone. And a precision-tuning tool that reports ``\texttt{fp32} suffices''
has not thereby predicted a speedup: on this evidence the same answer is worth a
$2.86\times$ or a $1.07\times$ speedup depending on a storage decision the tool
never sees. That gap is a concrete instance of the dual objective the original
tools tried to
optimize directly, and an argument for the factorization of
\S\ref{sec:observable}, in which precision search and cost measurement are kept
apart.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Precimonious}

Precimonious~\cite{precimonious} operates on LLVM bitcode. Given a program, a set
of test inputs, and an error threshold, it searches for a \emph{type
configuration}: an assignment of a floating-point type to each program variable.
The search is delta debugging, with worst-case complexity $O(n^2)$ in the number
of variables, and each candidate requires recompilation and re-execution. The
paper reports cases in which analysis takes hours for programs declaring fewer
than 50 variables with native runtimes under five seconds.

Two practical constraints deserve note because they persist in any successor.
First, the analysis is performed without optimization, because optimization
eliminates the variables the search ranges over --- so the configuration is
tuned on a program that differs from the one that ships. Second, the search is
empirical: a configuration meeting $10^{-6}$ will satisfy $10^{-4}$, but the
configuration found directly for $10^{-4}$ may differ, and may perform better.

\subsection{Blame Analysis}

Blame Analysis~\cite{blame} replaces search with attribution. Each floating-point
instruction is executed under shadow execution at several operand precisions
simultaneously --- \texttt{float}, \texttt{double}, and \texttt{double} truncated
to 4, 6, 8 and 10 significant decimal digits. For a given precision requirement
on an instruction's result, the analysis records the minimal operand precisions
that satisfy it; this \emph{blame set} is merged across dynamic instances of the
instruction by taking the maximum requirement. A backward pass from the target
statement then yields the set of variables that may be single precision.

The cost structure matters for anyone considering the approach at scale. The
online variant is bounded by the static instruction count and imposes up to
$50\times$ runtime overhead. The offline variant finds better configurations but
is bounded by the \emph{dynamic} trace: it exhausted 256\,GB of memory on NAS
\texttt{ep} at class~S, the smallest input designed for serial execution.

One remark in that paper is the germ of the present work's approach to scale: the
authors observe that long-running scientific programs generally use iterative
solvers, that precision requirements tighten toward the end of execution, and
that analyzing only the last few iterations is therefore likely to suffice. In
other words, deciding \emph{what} to analyze is the lever, and it is a modeling
decision rather than an algorithmic one.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Two orthogonal axes}

Prior tools bind their search strategy to their measurement technology.
Precimonious' delta debugging is inseparable from its LLVM~3.0 instrumentation;
PROMISE's search is inseparable from CADNA. The consequence is that neither
survives its dependency, and that a search improvement cannot be evaluated
against a different error model.

Parsimony defines two protocols:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
class Evaluator(Protocol):
    caps: Caps
    def discover(self) -> ConfigSpace: ...
    def evaluate(self, config: Config) -> Result: ...

class Search(Protocol):
    def search(self, space: ConfigSpace,
               evaluate: Callable[[Config], Result]) -> dict[str, Format]: ...
\end{lstlisting}

An \texttt{Evaluator} answers what a configuration costs in accuracy, and
optionally in time; a \texttt{Search} decides which configurations to ask about.
\texttt{Caps} declares what an evaluator can observe --- which formats, whether it
requires a rebuild, whether it sees GPU code, its slowdown --- so that a request no
installed evaluator can serve fails explicitly rather than being silently served
by something weaker.

The separation has a methodological payoff beyond modularity: it makes the search
testable in isolation. A synthetic oracle with a known minimal set exercises the
search with no floating-point emulation involved at all
(\S\ref{sec:validation}), so a regression in the search is diagnosed where it
occurs rather than surfacing as an inexplicably poor precision assignment.

\subsection{Formats and emulation}
\label{sec:emulation}

A format is an (exponent bits, significand bits) pair, which covers the IEEE
formats, the vendor formats, and \emph{virtual} widths --- a \texttt{float64}
truncated to $k$ significand bits with the full exponent range. Virtual widths
are how one asks how many bits a value needs without committing to a storage
format, and they generalize the truncated-decimal lattice Blame Analysis used
internally.

Rounding is emulated on \texttt{float64}: the significand is rounded to nearest
via \texttt{frexp}/\texttt{ldexp} scaling, values above the format's largest
finite magnitude become infinite, and values below its minimum normal exponent
are quantized to the appropriate subnormal step. This is exact for any format
whose range and precision fit within \texttt{float64}, which is every format
below it; wider formats require a native or instrumented evaluator.

\subsection{Search}
\label{sec:search}

The delta-debugging adaptation requires one observation to state correctly.
Delta debugging minimizes a subset subject to a predicate that holds on the full
set. Here the subset is the set of variables \emph{retained at higher precision},
and the predicate is that the accuracy goal is met. That predicate is monotone in
the direction the algorithm assumes --- returning a variable to higher precision
cannot hurt accuracy --- so the standard algorithm applies unmodified. Where
monotonicity fails, as when a coarser format is accidentally favorable to a
particular cancellation, the algorithm still terminates and the result is locally
rather than globally minimal, which is equally true of the original tools.

The search walks the format ladder from high to low. At each rung, the variables
still above that rung are candidates for demotion; delta debugging identifies the
minimal set that must remain above it; the remainder are demoted and the search
proceeds. This costs $O(n^2)$ evaluations \emph{per rung}, which is the dominant
scalability concern and the motivation for the prefilter of
\S\ref{sec:prefilter}.

We also provide uniform bisection over the ladder, which finds the lowest single
format that suffices in $O(\log |\text{ladder}|)$ evaluations. This is not a
competitor to the mixed search but a control for it: the gap between the uniform
floor and the mixed configuration is the only honest measure of what the
expensive search bought.

\subsection{A cheap prefilter}
\label{sec:prefilter}

Blame Analysis established the pattern of pruning the space with a cheap analysis
before searching it, and reported the combination running $9\times$ faster on
average. Its mechanism --- shadow execution at instruction granularity, at up to
$50\times$ runtime --- is too expensive for the scales we care about. We use a
much coarser one at the granularity the search already ranges over.

For each variable $v$, bisect the ladder to find the lowest format at which
lowering \emph{$v$ alone}, with every other variable at full precision, still
meets the goal. That format becomes $v$'s floor; variables that cannot be lowered
even on their own are removed from the search entirely. The cost is $O(n \log L)$
evaluations for $n$ variables and $L$ rungs.

The floor is a heuristic, not a bound, and the distinction is worth stating because
the tempting argument is false. One would like to say: if $v$ at format $f$ fails
with everything else at full precision, it cannot pass when other variables are
lowered too. That assumes lowering a variable only \emph{adds} error. But
rounding is a projection onto a lattice, so a coarse format downstream can
\emph{absorb} upstream error, snapping a badly wrong value onto a grid point near
the reference. We observed exactly this absorption (\S\ref{sec:threats}). The
prefilter may
therefore exclude configurations that would have passed, and can return a wider
assignment than an unfiltered search --- though in practice the excluded
configurations are frequently the ones that pass by accident.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{Emulation against hardware}

Emulated rounding to \texttt{fp32} and \texttt{fp16} is bit-identical to numpy's
native \texttt{float32} and \texttt{float16} over random samples, including
inputs in the subnormal range, and \texttt{bfloat16} is verified to retain
\texttt{fp32}'s exponent range on values that overflow \texttt{fp16}.

\subsection{Search against a synthetic oracle}

The delta-debugging implementation is tested against a predicate that holds
exactly when a known subset is present. The search recovers that subset exactly,
its output is verified 1-minimal, and the evaluation count is bounded well below
exhaustive enumeration. No floating-point emulation participates in these tests.

\subsection{Reproducing a published result}
\label{sec:repro}

The worked example of the Blame Analysis paper (its Figure~2) computes
$a^4 - 4a^3 + 6a^2 - 4a + 1$ at $a = 1.84089642$ by repeated multiplication.
Intermediate terms have magnitude up to roughly $25$ while the result is near
$0.5$, so the
summation loses about $1.7$ decimal digits to cancellation. The paper concludes
that only the variable \texttt{factor} inside \texttt{mpow} may be lowered,
because it holds small exact integers.

Parsimony selects the same variable independently: at a relative tolerance of
$10^{-8}$, one of eleven variables is lowered, and it is \texttt{factor}. Because
the ladder extends below \texttt{float}, the result is sharper than the original
tool could express --- \texttt{factor} holds $6$, $4$ and $1$, so it descends to an
8-bit format with \emph{zero} error.

The emulator also reproduces the paper's all-single figure, $0.4999980927$,
bit-for-bit against a native \texttt{float32} evaluation
($0.4999980926513672$).

\section{Conjugate Gradient}
\label{sec:nascg}

NAS \texttt{cg} is in Precimonious' benchmark set and is one of only five
programs in the Blame Analysis study showing any speedup, which makes it the
natural anchor. We tune a transcription of its inner loop, preserving the C
variable names, over three variable-coefficient diffusion operators of increasing
conditioning ($\kappa$ from $38$ to $211$). Iteration counts are calibrated per
case to the number of iterations \texttt{fp64} needs to reduce the relative residual
below $10^{-12}$, then held fixed across all configurations so that iteration count
is not a confounding variable. Two differences from the benchmark are worth
stating: the operator is a diffusion stencil rather than NAS' random sparse
matrix, and the tuned set is the algorithm's ten working-set quantities rather
than the 32 floating-point variables the C program declares. Variable
\emph{counts} are therefore not comparable; the character of the assignment is.

Table~\ref{tab:cg} shows a structured rather than uniform answer. The scalars
that \emph{steer} the iteration --- the step length $\alpha$, the conjugacy
coefficient $\beta$, and the residual norms $\rho, \rho_0$ --- sit at or below
\texttt{bfloat16} wherever the vectors are at \texttt{fp32}, and $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ are assigned 8 bits at $10^{-4}$. The quantities that \emph{carry state} ---
the operator and the vectors $r$, $z$, $q$ --- set the achievable accuracy. This
split is
the conjugate-gradient form of a familiar fact: an inaccurate step length yields a
slightly non-optimal step, which the next residual corrects, whereas error in the
accumulated solution does not self-correct. The search direction $p$ sits
consistently below $r$ and $z$ for the same reason.

The operator tracks the state vectors, at \texttt{fp32} for tolerances of
$10^{-6}$ and looser. Since sparse matrix-vector products are bandwidth-bound,
the operator's format is the storage decision with the largest performance
consequence, and the
answer is not trivially full precision.

Against the published result, agreement is partial. Expressed as the fraction of
variables lowered below double, Blame\,+\,Precimonious reports $94\%$, $59\%$,
$50\%$, $50\%$ at the four thresholds, against $100\%$, $100\%$, $60\%$, $0\%$
here. Both lower nearly everything at $10^{-4}$ and about half at $10^{-8}$. The
divergence at $10^{-10}$ is a property of the acceptance criterion rather than
the search, discussed in \S\ref{sec:criterion}.

The prefilter cuts $214$, $229$, $306$ and $294$ evaluations to $35$, $36$, $27$
and $20$ --- $6.1\times$ to $14.7\times$ --- while increasing storage by $7.7\%$,
$1.3\%$, $6.9\%$ and $0\%$. This is comparable to the $9\times$ average that Blame
Analysis reported for its own prefilter, and it is obtained here without shadow
execution.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

Three experimental-design errors arose in producing Table~\ref{tab:cg}. Each
yielded a plausible-looking result that was meaningless, and each is easy to make.

\paragraph{An operator whose entries are exactly representable.} The first
version used a constant-coefficient five-point Laplacian, with entries $4$ and
$-1$. Both are exact in every format down to 8 bits, so $A$ came back at 8 bits
at \emph{every} tolerance including $10^{-10}$. Read quickly, this is a striking
finding about matrix storage; it is in fact the observation that rounding a no-op
changes nothing. Generic entries are required for the storage question to be
meaningful at all.

\paragraph{An uncalibrated iteration count.} Running a fixed $120$ iterations on
problems converging in $31$ to $76$ left the well-conditioned cases iterating in
the noise floor, where $\rho = \lVert r \rVert^2$ underflows at low precision and
the run returns NaN. The search was then partly measuring underflow in dead
iterations rather than solver accuracy.

\paragraph{A single input.} On the \texttt{mpow} example of \S\ref{sec:repro},
the result at $a = 1.84089642$ lies within $2.3 \times 10^{-8}$ of exactly
$0.5$, which an 8-bit format represents exactly. Given only that input, the
search satisfies a $10^{-4}$ tolerance by coarsening the \emph{output} until it
quantizes onto that lattice point --- a downstream format absorbing upstream error,
rather than an accurate computation. This is also the concrete counterexample to
the monotonicity argument of \S\ref{sec:prefilter}: the input variable $a$ alone
at 8 bits fails, yet a configuration with $a$ at 8 bits passes. Evaluating over
several inputs removes the accident and restores monotonicity in tolerance.
Precimonious notes the need for representative inputs and treats their generation
as orthogonal; these results suggest it is not optional.

\subsection{A limitation of the full-precision reference}
\label{sec:criterion}

At $10^{-10}$ every variable returns to \texttt{fp64}, and the assignment
persists after
tightening the reference's own convergence to $7 \times 10^{-13}$, so it is not a
convergence artifact. Reduced-precision CG does not converge to the same iterate
as \texttt{fp64} CG; it converges to a nearby but different point. Requiring
agreement with the \texttt{fp64} answer to ten digits therefore requires
reproducing a particular rounding trajectory, not merely solving the problem
well. As the tolerance approaches the solver's own convergence level, an
acceptance criterion phrased as relative error against a full-precision run stops
measuring solution quality and begins measuring bit-reproduction.

For iterative solvers, a criterion phrased on the residual, or on error against
the exact solution where it is known, is better posed. This is a change to the
acceptance criterion rather than to the search, and it applies to this whole
family of tools, Precimonious and PROMISE included.

\section{Newton's Method: How Many Bits Does a Jacobian Need?}
\label{sec:petsc}

\subsection{The observable}
\label{sec:observable}

Practitioners know that inexact Newton methods tolerate inaccurate Jacobians;
nobody has published a number, and the reason is that the question is not well
posed until one decides what to measure. Write the step with a stored, rounded
Jacobian as $s = -(J+E)^{-1}F$. Then
\[
  F + Js = E\,(J+E)^{-1}F, \qquad
  \eta := \frac{\lVert F + Js\rVert}{\lVert F\rVert} \lesssim \lVert E\rVert\,\lVert J^{-1}\rVert \le u_p\,\kappa(J),
\]
which is the forcing term of Dembo--Eisenstat--Steihaug inexact Newton with
$u_p = 2^{-p}$. Standard theory then says two things. The converged solution does
not depend on $J$'s precision at all, provided $F$ is evaluated accurately and the
iteration converges: $J$ chooses the direction, $F$ is what is driven to zero.
What changes is the \emph{rate}, from quadratic to q-linear with contraction
$\approx \eta$. An error-based criterion would therefore report that the Jacobian
needs zero bits --- true, and useless.

The observable is cost at fixed accuracy: hold the residual tolerance fixed, as a
solver does anyway, and count iterations. This inverts the Precimonious framing.
It also generalizes: every quantity in an iterative method that is not the
residual or the solution --- a preconditioner, a search direction, a step --- is a
fixed operator that changes the iteration count and cannot change the fixed
point. Quantities divide into \emph{rate-limiting} and \emph{accuracy-limiting},
and one criterion catches both: converged to tolerance within an iteration
budget. Rate-limiting cost appears as extra iterations; accuracy-limiting failure
appears as non-convergence.

\subsection{Setup and predictions}

We solve the Bratu problem $-\Delta u - \lambda e^u = 0$ on the unit square with
a five-point stencil and Newton with a direct linear solve --- PETSc's SNES
\texttt{ex5}~\cite{petsc} in its simplest form --- over three cases spanning a
decade in conditioning: $(k,\lambda) = (16,1)$, $(16,6)$, $(24,6)$ with
$\kappa(J) = 123, 265, 575$ at the solution. Variables are the stored Jacobian
$J$, step $\delta$, residual $F$ and iterate $u$; a second configuration rounds
the two terms of $F$ separately \emph{before} they are subtracted, so the
cancellation in $-\Delta u - \lambda e^u$ is modeled. Convergence is judged by an
independent \texttt{fp64} residual of the returned iterate, for a reason given
below.

The predictions were written down before running anything. $J$ and $\delta$ are
rate-limiting with knee near $\log_2 \kappa + 3 \approx 10$--$12$ bits. The
stored $F$ perturbs the right-hand side relatively, giving $\eta \approx u_p$
with \emph{no} $\kappa$: nearly free. The stored $u$ caps attainable accuracy at
$u_p\kappa$, so it needs $\log_2\kappa + \log_2(1/\mathrm{rtol}) \approx 40$--$42$
bits and the threshold should \emph{move with $\kappa$}. The residual terms
before cancellation have a floor $\approx u_p$ relative to the terms' magnitude,
so they need $\log_2(1/\mathrm{rtol}) + 3 \approx 36$ bits and the threshold
should \emph{not} move with $\kappa$. The cases were chosen to make that last
distinction observable.

Table~\ref{tab:bratu-thresholds} summarizes the single-variable sweep. Four of
five predictions held as mechanisms, not merely as numbers: the $u$ threshold
moves with $\kappa$ and the cancellation threshold does not, which is the
difference between an accuracy floor proportional to $u_p\kappa$ and one
proportional to $u_p$. A single case could not have shown that difference. The
fifth prediction was wrong in the most useful way.

\paragraph{The Jacobian degrades to Picard iteration.} The bound
$u_p\kappa \gtrsim 1$ predicts divergence below about 7 bits. Divergence never
occurs.
At low precision the diagonal entry $4/h^2 - \lambda e^{u}$ rounds to $4/h^2$
--- the nonlinear term falls below the ulp of the Laplacian's diagonal --- so the
stored Jacobian degenerates to the discrete Laplacian $L$, and Newton's update
$u \leftarrow u - L^{-1}(Lu - \lambda e^u) = L^{-1}\lambda e^u$ \emph{is} Picard
iteration. Picard converges for $\lambda$ below the turning point
$\lambda^* \approx 6.81$ at a rate set by $\lambda/\lambda^*$, which is why the
low-precision floor is 8 iterations at $\lambda=1$ and 39 at $\lambda=6$
regardless of grid; pure Picard reproduces the floors exactly (8, 39, 39 against
8, 39, 40). So the answer to the section's question is: about 10 bits for this
problem, and below that one pays Picard's rate rather than failing. The knee is
where the smallest physically meaningful term in a row is resolved against the
largest --- $\log_2$ of the row's dynamic range --- which is the diagnostic to
carry to a stiff or multiphysics Jacobian.

\paragraph{False convergence.} With the residual's terms rounded before
cancellation, the solver's own stopping test fires spuriously: near the solution
$\Delta_h u \approx \lambda e^u$, both terms round to the same representable
value, the computed residual is exactly zero, and the solver declares victory.
In the raw sweep this appeared as ``converged in one or two iterations'' at 4--8
bits. A real solver has only its own residual to consult. Mixed-precision
iterative refinement evaluates the residual in high precision for this
reason~\cite{carsonhigham}; we rediscovered the rule from the failure side, and
the tool now enforces it: a kernel's convergence flag must come from an
independent high-precision residual.

Table~\ref{tab:bratu-pareto} gives the mixed-precision search under an
iteration budget. \textbf{Storing the Jacobian in \texttt{fp32} is free}: zero
iteration cost. \texttt{fp16} storage costs a third more iterations. No format
below \texttt{fp16} is feasible at any budget tested, for two different reasons
worth separating: \texttt{fp8e4m3} overflows the stencil entries
($4/h^2 \approx 1156 > 448$), while \texttt{fp8e5m2}'s two significand bits put
the solver on Picard's rate, eight times the iterations. The iterate $u$ is
\texttt{fp64} at every budget, as an accuracy-limiting quantity must be.

The right-hand table is the storage-versus-arithmetic distinction as a single
row: the residual may be \emph{stored} in 16 bits while its terms may not be
\emph{evaluated} in fewer than 33, and the real-format ladder has nothing between
\texttt{fp32}'s 24 and \texttt{fp64}'s 53. A tool that models storage precision
must not be read as answering an arithmetic-precision question; this is the
concrete demonstration of the scope statement in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

At the three budgets, the prefilter reduces 72, 80 and 55 evaluations to 13, 18
and 14, for 0--5\% more storage.

\subsection{Putting the two tiers together}

The design factorizes time as iterations $\times$ cost per iteration, and both
factors are now measured. Storing the Jacobian in \texttt{fp32} costs no
iterations (Table~\ref{tab:bratu-pareto}) and, in a blocked layout, speeds each
matrix-vector product up by $1.75\times$ against blocked \texttt{fp64}, or by
$2.86\times$ against the scalar \texttt{fp64} a solver is likely to be using
(Table~\ref{tab:spmv}). Those factors multiply: the same accuracy in a little
over a third of the time per product.

The composition also shows where the two tiers disagree, which is the useful
part. Tier one says \texttt{fp16} Jacobian storage costs a third more iterations
and is otherwise viable; tier two says \texttt{fp16} recovers only $58\%$ of its
predicted bandwidth gain. Multiplying, blocked \texttt{fp16} speeds each product
up by
$2.30\times$ but costs $1.33\times$ the iterations --- a net $1.73\times$,
slightly \emph{worse} than \texttt{fp32}'s $1.75\times$ at no iteration cost.
Neither tier alone supports that conclusion, and a search minimizing modeled time
would have had to be told the storage layout to find it.

Three things in Table~\ref{tab:e2e}. First, the iteration count is \emph{exactly}
unchanged --- by precision, by storage layout, and by whether the Jacobian was
assembled on the host or the device --- with displacements agreeing to five or
six digits. The Bratu prediction of \S\ref{sec:petsc} holds on a production
elasticity solver.

Second, and this is the result the extracted kernels could not have produced,
\textbf{the larger gain is in assembly, not the solve}. Finite element assembly
--- quadrature and tensor contraction --- is compute-bound, whereas the sparse
matrix-vector product is bandwidth-bound; \texttt{fp32} cuts both the arithmetic
and the traffic and returns a steady $1.54$--$1.55\times$, against
$1.26$--$1.30\times$ for the solve. The solve figure is what the byte model of
\S\ref{sec:intro} predicts for a scalar layout, which is a useful consistency
check on the model; the assembly figure is outside the model entirely. Composing
the tiers at $1.48$M degrees of freedom: zero extra iterations, $1.54\times$ on
assembly, $1.30\times$ on the solve, about $1.4\times$ overall for the same
answer.

Third, the blocked operator is \emph{slower} here, and the reason is instructive
rather than anomalous. Its \texttt{MatMult} takes $1.49\times$ the time of the
scalar one in \texttt{fp64}. That is the expected single-GPU behaviour, not a
defect: Adams~\cite{baijkokkos} measures the same deficit on the same library and
explains it. The blocked format's advantage is compressed column indices, so it
is realized only when there is halo communication to amortize the index traffic
against; on one GPU the tuned scalar kernel wins. That work reports the blocked
kernel
taking $1.44\times$ (Q1) and $1.62\times$ (Q2) the time of the scalar one on one
GPU, and running $1.42\times$ \emph{faster} than it on 27 GPUs. Our $1.49\times$
at Q2 on one GPU sits squarely in that range, and our byte accounting agrees
with theirs to the digit: $1.41\times$ against $1.42\times$ for the
traffic-bound ceiling on blocking in \texttt{fp64}. Single-GPU measurements of a
blocked format therefore say nothing about its behaviour at scale, which is
worth stating because the temptation to
generalize from one device is strong.

What does not follow from that work, because it measures only \texttt{fp64}, is
the precision behaviour: going from \texttt{fp64} to \texttt{fp32} gives the
blocked kernel a speedup of $0.81\times$ --- it slows down --- while the scalar
kernel speeds up by $1.30\times$. The byte model predicts the opposite. At
\texttt{fp64} the index is 4 of the 12 bytes per scalar nonzero and blocking
saves $30\%$ of the traffic; at \texttt{fp32} the values shrink to 4 bytes, the
index share rises, and blocking should save $45\%$, raising the traffic-bound
ceiling on blocking from $1.42\times$ to $1.80\times$. That the measurement
moves the other way says the blocked kernel
has left the bandwidth-bound regime at four-byte values --- the same transition
\S\ref{sec:intro} found for \texttt{fp16} in the standalone kernel, arriving one
format earlier because blocking has already removed most of the traffic.

We report this because it is the sharpest illustration available of this paper's
own thesis. The payoff of a precision decision is a property of the code and the
machine configuration that execute it: the same \texttt{fp32} recommendation is
worth $1.30\times$, nothing, or a slowdown depending on the storage format, the
GPU count, and whether the kernel is still bandwidth-bound. A tuning tool
reporting ``\texttt{fp32} suffices'' predicts none of that.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Search cost.} Delta debugging costs $O(n^2)$ evaluations per ladder
rung. The prefilter of \S\ref{sec:prefilter} reduces this by $6$--$15\times$ on
the problems here, but both remain superlinear in variables against an evaluator
that may be arbitrarily expensive, and neither has been exercised beyond tens of
variables. A hierarchical scheme in the manner of HiFPTuner~\cite{hifptuner},
grouping variables before searching, is the natural next step.

\paragraph{Prefilter floors are heuristic.} The per-variable floors are not sound
bounds, for the reason given in \S\ref{sec:prefilter} and demonstrated in
\S\ref{sec:threats}: coarse rounding downstream can absorb upstream error, so a
variable that cannot be lowered alone may be lowerable in combination. The
prefilter can therefore return a wider assignment than an unfiltered search. We
report both routes rather than presenting the cheap one as equivalent.

\paragraph{No performance measurement in the search loop.} The numpy evaluator
answers how many bits the arithmetic requires, never whether a configuration is
faster. The timings of Tables~\ref{tab:spmv} and~\ref{tab:e2e} come from
separately written kernels and uniform builds, not from the tuning loop, so the
search cannot minimize measured time. The dual objective that motivates this
paper requires an evaluator that compiles and times real code, and none exists
yet.

\paragraph{Granularity at scale.} Instruction-level attribution cost $50\times$
runtime and, in its offline form, 256\,GB on a small benchmark. Applying it to a
production library is not plausible. We believe the tractable variant is
attribution at the granularity of \emph{library operations} --- a sparse
matrix-vector product, an inner product --- rather than LLVM instructions. This
trades resolution for feasibility, and it shifts the hard step to deciding which
operations to attribute, which is a modeling judgment informed by reading the
source.

\paragraph{Storage, not arithmetic.} The numpy evaluator rounds \emph{stored}
quantities: the result of each named computation at the point it is named. It
does not model arithmetic performed in reduced precision --- a dot product
accumulated in \texttt{bfloat16}, or a residual's terms evaluated in
\texttt{fp16} before they cancel. For storage formats, which is where the
bandwidth is, the model is exact. For the scalars the CG study found tolerant to
8 bits, the honest statement is ``these need not be \emph{accurate}'', not
``these may be \emph{computed} in 8-bit arithmetic''; they will be computed in
registers regardless, at no cost, and the finding has no performance content of
its own. \S\ref{sec:petsc} makes the gap concrete: the same residual may be
stored in 16 bits and may not be evaluated in fewer than 33.

\paragraph{The optimization gap, mostly sidestepped.} Tuning at \texttt{-O0} and
shipping at \texttt{-O3} is a hazard for tools whose variables are compiler
temporaries
that optimization deletes or keeps in registers. Our variables are algorithmic
quantities --- a stored matrix, a residual vector --- that survive optimization
by construction because they live in memory. What remains is the gap of the previous
paragraph: fused or extended-precision arithmetic on scalars differs from
per-operation rounding, which matters for reductions and for nothing else here.

\paragraph{Accelerator code.} LLVM-instrumentation evaluators do not observe GPU
kernels, which is where the reduced-precision payoff is largest. Conclusions must
presently be established on a CPU path and confirmed empirically on the
accelerator.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Search-based tuning.} Precimonious~\cite{precimonious} and Blame
Analysis~\cite{blame} are the direct ancestors. HiFPTuner~\cite{hifptuner} adds
static analysis to group intermediates and search hierarchically.
TAFFO~\cite{taffo} targets fixed-point conversion. POP~\cite{pop} takes a static,
constraint-based route.

\paragraph{Parallel revival.} PROMISE has recently been extended with FloatX to
support \texttt{bfloat16} and both 8-bit encodings, using CADNA's discrete
stochastic arithmetic for validation~\cite{promise2026}. That work draws the
same conclusion as ours about the value of revisiting these methods for modern
formats, from an independent lineage, and reports that $43\%$ of variables admit
32 bits or fewer and $28\%$ admit 16 or fewer. Its benchmarks remain small and
serial; the gap at HPC scale is what motivates the present design.

\paragraph{Error estimation.} Verificarlo~\cite{verificarlo} instruments LLVM IR
to provide Monte Carlo arithmetic and variable-precision backends;
Verrou~\cite{verrou} achieves comparable ends via Valgrind without recompilation;
CADNA~\cite{cadna} implements discrete stochastic arithmetic through instrumented
types. All three are candidate evaluators under the protocol of
\S\ref{sec:design}.

\paragraph{Mixed precision by analysis rather than search.} Where a numerical
method's error is understood analytically, precision can be assigned by theory
rather than by search --- iterative refinement in three precisions being the
canonical example~\cite{carsonhigham}. The two approaches are complementary:
theory covers the kernel, search covers the surrounding code that no one has
analyzed.

\section{Conclusion}

Search-based precision tuning was built for hardware that did not reward it. The
evidence is in its own evaluations: four of ten programs in the Blame Analysis
study show exactly $0.0\%$ speedup at every threshold, not because the search
failed to find lower-precision configurations --- it found them, covering $40\%$
of variables on average --- but because converting \texttt{double} to
\texttt{float} on a 2013 CPU was worth almost nothing.

Rebuilding these methods against \texttt{bfloat16}, 8-bit formats and virtual bit
widths yields answers the original lattice could not express. On conjugate
gradient the assignment is structured along a line practitioners would recognize
but have not quantified: the scalars that steer an iteration tolerate 8 to 16
bits, while the operator and the vectors that accumulate the solution set the
achievable accuracy. The operator admits \texttt{fp32} storage to $10^{-6}$,
which for a bandwidth-bound kernel is where the time is.

We also report what went wrong. Three experimental-design errors each produced a
plausible result that meant nothing --- an operator whose entries were exactly
representable, an uncalibrated iteration count that measured underflow in dead
iterations, and a single input that let the search satisfy its tolerance by
quantizing the output onto a lucky lattice point. Any tool in this family can
produce these, and the third also refutes the natural soundness argument for
prefiltering.

For Newton's method the question ``how many bits does the Jacobian need'' has an
answer once the observable is chosen correctly: iteration count at fixed
residual tolerance. Under it, \texttt{fp32} storage of the Jacobian is free,
\texttt{fp16} costs a third more iterations, and below about ten bits the
iteration does not diverge but degrades to Picard's rate, because the nonlinear
term falls below the ulp of the stencil's diagonal. The same experiment shows
that storage precision and arithmetic precision are different questions --- the
residual stores in 16 bits and evaluates in no fewer than 33 --- and that a
solver evaluating its residual in low precision will report false convergence
unless convergence is judged by an independent high-precision residual.

Finally, the payoff. We set out to show that the hardware had changed enough to
make these methods worth reviving, and the measurement does not say quite that.
Blocked \texttt{fp32} operator storage costs no iterations and runs each product
$2.86\times$ faster than scalar \texttt{fp64}, which is the result we hoped for.
But scalar \texttt{fp32} on a seven-point stencil yields a $1.07\times$ speedup
--- $19\%$ of what
the byte model allows, and indistinguishable from the $0.0\%$ the 2013 evaluation
reported. On the data structures the original tools targeted, their conclusion
still holds.

What changed is therefore not only the hardware but the format. Reduced precision
pays when the index traffic it cannot reduce has already been amortized by
blocking, and elasticity, multi-species transport and any other multi-component
physics amortize it by construction. That is a narrower claim than the one we
started with and a more useful one, because it says where to look. It also says
what a precision-tuning tool cannot do on its own: the same recommendation is
worth a $2.86\times$ or a $1.07\times$ speedup depending on a storage decision
outside its model, which is why we factorize the objective rather than search on it.

The end-to-end check (\S\ref{sec:e2e}) closes the loop: complete single- and
double-precision builds of a production elasticity solver, same iteration counts,
same answer, about $1.4\times$ faster. And it adds a finding that kernel measurements
alone could not have produced --- that the larger share of that gain is in
finite element assembly, which is compute-bound, rather than in the
bandwidth-bound solve, the usual first target.

What remains open is not a measurement but a scope. Every result here concerns
\emph{storage} precision, on a CPU-emulated model for the search tiers and on two
uniformly built libraries for the end-to-end check. Mixed-precision arithmetic
within a kernel, a per-variable assignment compiled into a real solver rather
than a uniform build, and the blocked device kernel's anomalous behaviour in
\texttt{fp32} are each a separate piece of work.

\section*{Use of AI}

Anthropic's Claude models, operating agentically through Claude Code, were used
under the author's direction for background research and exploration, analysis,

manuscript. The author made all design and experimental decisions, verified all

Two episodes are worth recording, because they bear on how such tools should be
used for measurement rather than on authorship. A per-event GPU profile was
initially mis-attributed --- asynchronous kernel launches credited the
synchronizing reduction with the sparse matrix-vector product's time --- and was
caught only because the implied bandwidth, half a percent of the device peak, was
impossible; the affected numbers were discarded and remeasured with forced
per-event synchronization. Separately, a single-GPU blocked-format deficit was
initially reported here as an implementation defect before being identified as
the documented and expected behaviour at that scale~\cite{baijkokkos}. Both
corrections came from checking a number against a physical bound or against prior
work, not from the tooling.
